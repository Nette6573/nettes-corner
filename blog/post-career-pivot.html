Starting Over When Everyone Says You Can't:
A Career Pivot Survival Guide for Women

By Nette
August 6, 2026
10 min read

Someone in your life has probably already said it to you, maybe kindly, maybe not. You're too far in now. That's a big risk to take at your age. Why would you throw away everything you've already built. Maybe it was a parent, a manager, a friend who meant well but could not picture you as anything other than who you already were to them. Maybe the loudest voice saying it was your own, at two in the morning, staring at a screen full of course material that made no sense yet.

I am writing this in the middle of my own pivot, still working full time in a completely different field while I build toward a new one. But this is not really a post about me breaking into tech. It is for every woman standing where I am standing, in whatever field she is trying to walk into, being told by someone, anyone, that she cannot, and needing a reason to believe otherwise.

You Are Not the First Woman to Hear "It's Too Late"

Vera Wang spent her childhood on the ice, chasing a spot on the 1968 Olympic figure skating team, and did not make it. She spent the next seventeen years at Vogue, working her way up, and was passed over for the editor in chief role she had been building toward the entire time. She did not enter fashion design, the field she is now famous for defining, until she was forty years old, with no formal design training. Looking back on the fear of that decision, she has said plainly: "Success isn't about the end result; it's about what you learn along the way."

Julia Child spent years in advertising, media, and wartime intelligence work before she ever set foot in a professional kitchen. She did not publish her first cookbook until she was fifty. She once said the entire obstacle standing between people and the thing they actually want to do is simple: "The only real stumbling block is fear of failure." Not lack of talent. Not being too late. Fear, and only fear.

Toni Morrison worked for years as a teacher and an editor before she published her first novel at forty, and went on to become the first Black woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. And Sara Blakely, who built Spanx into a billion dollar company after years of unrelated work selling office equipment door to door, was raised by a father who deliberately reframed failure for her early on, until she came to believe, in her own words, "Failure is not the outcome, failure is not trying."

"The only real stumbling block is fear of failure." — Julia Child

The Part Nobody Tells You: You Have to Grieve the Old Path First

Change management researcher William Bridges spent decades studying how people move through transitions, not the external event of a new job or a new title, but the internal, psychological process underneath it. His model names three stages, and the first one surprised me when I first read it: every transition begins with an ending, not a beginning. You have to let go of the identity, the routine, the sense of competence you built in your old path, before you can fully step into a new one.

I did not expect to grieve a job I was actively still doing. But there were quiet losses I had to name: the confidence of being the person who already knew exactly what she was doing, the comfort of a role people understood the moment I described it, the version of my future I had assumed for years without ever really choosing it. Bridges is clear that rushing past this stage does not make a transition go faster. It just buries feelings that resurface later as doubt, or fatigue, or the sense that something is wrong with you for still feeling shaky months in. If you are grieving a version of your life you have not fully let go of yet, nothing is wrong with you. You are simply doing the first, necessary part of the work.

The Neutral Zone Is the Part Nobody Warns You About

Bridges calls the middle stretch the neutral zone, the in between time where the old identity is gone but the new one is not fully built yet. This is where most transitions actually happen, and it is genuinely one of the harder places to stand. You are not purely who you used to be anymore, not in your own head, but you are also not yet who you are becoming in any way the world outside your own effort would recognize.

This is the season of studying after a full, physically or emotionally draining day at the job you have not left yet. Of passing an exam, or finishing a course, or landing a first small win in the new direction, and still going back to the old desk on Monday because the new title has not caught up to the new reality yet. The neutral zone does not feel like progress most days. It feels like limbo. But Bridges makes a point worth holding onto: the neutral zone is not wasted time, it is the actual site where the psychological realignment happens. The confusion is not a sign you are doing it wrong. It is the work.

What Actually Helps You Stay In It

A few things, none of them glamorous. Breaking a long, undefined goal into a sequence you can actually see and check off, one concrete marker at a time, instead of staring at the whole, foggy distance ahead of you. Applying, trying, or showing up before you feel fully ready, because waiting to feel ready is often just another way of staying in the neutral zone longer than you need to. And being honest with yourself about the exhaustion instead of pretending a full life and a new direction do not cost something real, they do, and that cost does not mean you chose wrong.

Vera Wang, decades into her own success, still describes it as a daily struggle, not a finished feeling of arrival. If a woman with a global fashion empire still calls it a struggle, you are allowed to call your own pivot hard without that meaning you have failed at it.

When the People Around You Say You Can't

Here is what I want to say directly to the doubters in your life, even the well meaning ones. Their doubt is often not really about you. It is about their own picture of what is possible, built from their own fears, their own timelines, their own version of what a safe life looks like. That picture was never required to be accurate about you. Sara Blakely's own advice on this is blunt and worth holding onto: be careful who you look to for validation on a new idea, because out of love and concern, the people closest to you will often hand you every reason it cannot work, before you have even had the chance to try.

That does not mean cutting people off. It means recognizing that their fear is not evidence, any more than your own fear is evidence. It is just fear, wearing the costume of wisdom.

The New Beginning Does Not Arrive All at Once

The final stage in Bridges' model, the new beginning, is not a single dramatic moment. It builds slowly: the first time you understand something in the new field without translating it in your head first, the first time someone treats your growing competence as a given instead of a work in progress, the first quiet morning you realize you have stopped explaining your choice to anyone, including yourself. It accumulates, then all at once it is simply who you are.

Try This This Week

Write down, in your own handwriting, every specific voice that has told you it is too late, whose voice it actually was, and one true fact about your own life that argues against it. Seeing the doubt in your own handwriting, next to the actual evidence, tends to shrink it faster than simply thinking about it does.

Journal Corner

What would I be attempting right now if no one in my life were watching or grading the outcome. Whose timeline have I quietly been measuring my own life against, and is that comparison even a fair one. And a year from today, what do I want to be able to say I did with this exact season of uncertainty, instead of just surviving it.

If someone has told you it is too late, too risky, or too far outside what they ever expected of you, I want you to remember that every woman named in this post heard some version of that too. You are not behind. You are in the middle of something real, and the fog you are standing in right now is not proof you chose wrong. It is proof you are actually doing it. — Nette
