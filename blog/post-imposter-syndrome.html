Imposter Syndrome Isn't Lying to You,
It's Just Outdated

By Nette
August 6, 2026
8 min read

I passed Security Plus and felt like a fraud the very next morning. Not for a moment, not for an hour. For days. I kept the certificate confirmation open in a browser tab like I needed to keep checking it was real, and even then some quiet, stubborn voice in the back of my mind kept insisting I had gotten lucky, that the questions had happened to line up with the handful of things I actually understood, that a real cybersecurity person would have felt different walking out of that exam than I did.

If you have ever earned something and immediately started explaining it away, you already know the voice I mean. It has a name, decades of research behind it, and a real, structured way out. I want to walk through all three.

Where the Term Actually Comes From

In 1978, psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes published the first research on what they called the impostor phenomenon, based on their clinical work with more than 150 high achieving women. What struck them was not that these women lacked competence. Quite the opposite. Every one of them had real, visible, well documented success behind her, degrees, awards, professional recognition. What they had in common instead was an internal experience of intellectual phoniness that no external evidence seemed able to touch. They did not feel like frauds because they were unqualified. They felt like frauds despite being unmistakably qualified.

That distinction matters more than it might seem. Imposter syndrome was never really a competence problem being mistaken for a confidence problem. It was, from the very first study, a mismatch between what a person had actually achieved and what their internal narrative was still willing to believe about themselves.

The feeling is not evidence. It is a lagging indicator.

The Five Shapes It Takes

Decades after the original study, psychologist Valerie Young built on that research and identified five distinct patterns in how imposter feelings actually show up, patterns she calls competence types. The Perfectionist believes anything short of flawless is proof of failure, and rarely pauses to credit the effort behind a real success. The Natural Genius expects to master new skills instantly, and reads the normal struggle of learning something new as evidence they do not belong. The Soloist believes asking for help is itself an admission of fraud, and pushes through alone rather than risk being seen needing support. The Expert feels they can never know enough, and keeps collecting credentials or information before they will let themselves feel ready. And the Superwoman or Superman pattern pushes to excel in every single role at once, using constant achievement to outrun the underlying fear of being exposed.

Most people recognize themselves in more than one. I see the Perfectionist and the Expert in myself most clearly, the two patterns that keep whispering that one more certification, one more perfect exam score, will finally be the one that makes the feeling stop. It never is, because the feeling was never really about the credentials in the first place.

What Actually Combats It

Dr. Young's own guidance, drawn from decades of coaching people through this exact pattern, starts with two deceptively simple steps. The first is breaking the silence. Shame is what keeps imposter feelings alive in isolation, and simply naming the feeling out loud to someone else, discovering that people you admire have felt it too, tends to shrink it immediately. The second is separating feelings from fact. There will be moments you feel unqualified. That feeling is real, but it is not information about your actual competence, and treating it as though it were is the entire trap.

Beyond those two starting points, a few practices have made a real difference for me directly. Keeping a written, dated record of things I have actually done, certifications passed, problems solved, feedback received, so the evidence exists outside my own unreliable memory of how I felt in the moment I earned it. Catching myself at the exact moment I am about to explain away a real win with luck or timing, and refusing to let the explaining happen unchallenged. And building relationships with people a few steps ahead of me in the field, because proximity to people who have survived the same doubt is one of the fastest ways to stop believing you are the only one who has ever felt it.

Why "Just Be Confident" Doesn't Work

This is why the usual advice, just believe in yourself, just be more confident, tends to fall flat on its own. You cannot argue your way out of a feeling by trying to feel a different feeling harder. What actually shifts the pattern is updating the evidence your internal narrative is working from, deliberately, and often repeatedly, because the old story does not get replaced in one sitting, one exam, or one win. It gets replaced slowly, the same way it was built.

Try This This Week

Start an actual evidence file today, even if it is just a note on your phone. Every time you finish something real, a course, a hard conversation, a task you were dreading, write the date and one line about what you did. The next time the voice tells you that you got lucky, you will have a running record that says otherwise, in your own words, dated and specific.

Journal Corner

Which of Valerie Young's five patterns do I see most clearly in myself, and what rule has that pattern been quietly holding me to. What is one recent success I explained away, and what would it sound like to describe it honestly instead. And who could I tell about this feeling, this week, so it stops living only in my own head.

If you are sitting with a certificate, a new title, or a real accomplishment, and some old, outdated voice is already working to explain it away, notice that the voice is not new information. It is just old information that has not caught up yet. Name it, write down the evidence, and let the facts outvote the feeling. — Nette
