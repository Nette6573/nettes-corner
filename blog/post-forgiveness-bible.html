What the Bible Actually Says About Forgiveness
(And Why It's So Hard)

By Nette
August 6, 2026
7 min read

In my last post, I talked about the betrayal I have been walking through, from a friend, and from someone I was in a relationship with, and how bitterness can sneak up on you even on the days you thought you had made peace with it. I promised I would follow that with something a lot of Christian content skips over: what scripture actually says about forgiveness, without smoothing over how hard it really is.

Because here is the thing. A lot of well meaning people will tell you to "just forgive and move on" and quote a verse at you like it is a magic switch. It is not. The Bible itself does not present forgiveness as easy. It presents it as costly, repeated, and often a lifelong discipline rather than a one time decision.

The Command Is Bigger Than We Want It to Be

When Peter asked Jesus how many times he was required to forgive someone who wronged him, offering seven times as if that was generous, Jesus answered: "I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:21-22, KJV).

That number is not really a number. Nobody is meant to count to four hundred and ninety and then stop. It is Jesus saying the ordinary human math of forgiveness, you get three strikes, I owe you nothing after what you did, does not apply here. And immediately after saying it, He tells the parable of a servant who was forgiven an impossible debt by his king, and then turned around and refused to forgive a much smaller debt owed to him, and it does not go well for that servant. The parable is not subtle: forgiveness is not optional generosity we extend when we feel like it. It is meant to flow out of how much we have already been forgiven.

Other places echo the same weight:

  • "Be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you" (Ephesians 4:32, KJV)
  • "Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another... even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye" (Colossians 3:13, KJV)
  • "For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you: but if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses" (Matthew 6:14-15, KJV)

That last one is sobering. Scripture ties our own forgiveness to our willingness to extend it. Not because God is keeping score in some petty way, but because an unforgiving heart and a heart that has truly received grace cannot coexist for long. One will eventually choke out the other.

"Not seven times, but seventy times seven." The math was never the point.

But Scripture Also Names How Ugly Unforgiveness Gets

This is the part that gets left out of a lot of sermons: the Bible does not pretend bitterness is a small thing, or that it stays contained. Hebrews warns believers to look diligently, "lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled" (Hebrews 12:15, KJV). A root. Something that grows underground, out of sight, until it pushes up through the surface and affects people who had nothing to do with the original wound, which, honestly, is exactly what displaced anger looks like in real life. Scripture saw that pattern long before psychology gave it a name.

Ephesians is even more specific: "Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice" (Ephesians 4:31, KJV), immediately followed by the verse on forgiveness. It is presented as one unit: the bitterness has to be actively put off, not just wished away, and forgiveness is what replaces it.

What Forgiveness Is Not, Biblically

This matters as much as what it is.

Forgiveness is not the same as trust. Trust is earned through demonstrated, consistent character over time. Even in scripture, reconciliation and restored relationship are treated as a separate, often slower process from the act of forgiving. God forgives immediately upon genuine repentance, but even scripture shows relationships taking time, evidence, and consistency to rebuild. Consider how long it took for the relationship between Joseph and his brothers to be fully restored in Genesis, even after forgiveness had already happened in his heart years earlier.

Forgiveness is not excusing. Nowhere does scripture say the wrong did not happen, did not matter, or was not real. Forgiveness in the Bible is offered because something genuinely wrong happened. That is the whole premise. You cannot forgive a debt that was never owed.

Forgiveness is not you taking on the job of punishing or acquitting. Romans says, "Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord" (Romans 12:19, KJV). Part of what forgiveness does, spiritually, is hand the accounting over to God instead of carrying it yourself. That is not the same as saying there are no consequences. It is saying the consequences are not yours to administer.

Forgiveness does not require pretending the relationship is unchanged. Even Jesus, teaching about a brother who sins against you, describes a process involving confrontation, and does not demand you act as though nothing happened (Luke 17:3-4; Matthew 18:15-17). Wisdom and forgiveness are not opposites.

Why It's Genuinely, Legitimately Hard

I do not think the Bible is naive about this. Jesus prayed "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34, KJV) while He was being executed, and even that moment does not read like it was effortless. It reads like a deliberate, costly act of will in the middle of real suffering, not a feeling that simply arrived on its own.

If you are finding this hard, if you can forgive with your mind on a good day and still feel your stomach tighten with rage on a bad one, that is not evidence you are failing at forgiveness or that your faith is weak. Even psychologists who have spent their careers studying forgiveness scientifically distinguish between a decision to forgive, which you can make in a moment, and the slower emotional unwinding of the hurt, which research shows can take much longer and often has to be revisited more than once. Scripture's repeated emphasis on "seventy times seven" suggests something similar, not that you will be betrayed four hundred and ninety times, but that forgiving the same wound may need to happen again and again as the memory resurfaces, and that is not failure. That is the actual shape of it.

Living in the Tension

So here is where I have landed, still very much in the middle of my own process. I can choose, today, to release the desire to see them punished, to hand the accounting to God instead of carrying it myself, and to refuse to let bitterness set up permanent residence in me. That is forgiveness, and it is a real, complete act even on the days it has to be renewed.

What I do not have to do is pretend the trust is fully back, or that every word from their mouth lands the same way it used to. Scripture never asked me to. It asked me to forgive as I have been forgiven, not to forget, not to be naive, and not to fast forward through a process that even Jesus treated as costly.

If you are walking through something like this, be honest with God about the anger rather than performing peace you do not feel yet. He can handle it. And be patient with yourself the way scripture is patient with the process, not seven times, but as many times as it takes. — Nette
