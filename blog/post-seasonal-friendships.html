Why Some Friendships Are Seasonal,
and That's Okay

By Nette
August 6, 2026
8 min read

Not every friendship I have lost ended in betrayal. Some of them just stopped. No fight, no falling out, no moment I could point to and say, that is where it broke. Just a slow fade, longer gaps between messages, plans that kept getting pushed until we both quietly stopped suggesting them, until one day I realized I had not spoken to someone who used to know almost everything about my life in the better part of a year. That kind of ending does not come with a villain. It still deserves to be grieved.

The Friendship That Did Not Explode, It Just Stopped

I used to feel almost embarrassed grieving these. It felt indulgent next to an actual betrayal, like mourning something that technically did not do anything wrong to me. But loss is still loss, even when nobody is at fault. Maybe especially then, because there is no one to be angry at, no clear story to tell yourself about what happened, just an absence with no explanation attached to it.

The Science of Fading

Anthropologist Robin Dunbar, known for his decades of research into how many stable relationships people can realistically maintain, has also studied what happens to friendships when contact drops off. His findings are almost clinical in how plainly they state something we feel but rarely say out loud: unlike family bonds, friendships decay without active investment. Close friendships need roughly weekly contact to stay close, and when that contact stops, closeness measurably erodes, typically within months, not years. Longitudinal research by Sam Roberts and Dunbar, tracking students through the disruption of leaving school, found the same pattern: friendships that stopped receiving investment declined in closeness, while family ties barely moved under the same disruption. Left long enough without renewed contact, research suggests it takes roughly three years for a once close friend to settle into simply being an acquaintance, someone you used to know.

None of that is a moral failure on anyone's part. It is closer to a resource allocation problem than a character problem. Life reroutes our time and attention constantly, new jobs, new cities, new seasons, and friendship, unlike family, does not have a default mechanism holding it together through the gaps. It survives on deliberate maintenance, and sometimes neither person has the capacity to maintain it, and neither one is the villain for that.

Friendships usually fade from neglect, not betrayal. That does not make the loss smaller.

What Others Have Said About This

There is a well loved, often shared poem, frequently attributed to Brian A. "Drew" Chalker, that puts language to exactly this feeling: people come into your life for a reason, a season, or a lifetime, and part of peace is learning to recognize which one you are in without demanding every relationship become the third. The writer C.S. Lewis, in his reflections on friendship, once described the exact moment a friendship is truly born as the moment one person discovers another who shares a private, particular way of seeing the world, and realizes they are not the only one who ever saw it that way. That is worth remembering when a friendship ends. Its purpose was never required to be forever to have been real while it lasted.

What Scripture Says About the Shape of Friendship

Ecclesiastes says plainly, "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven" (Ecclesiastes 3:1, KJV). I used to read that verse as being about grief and joy, mourning and dancing, the obvious pairs listed right after it. I did not used to read it as being about people, about the friends who were exactly right for a particular chapter of my life and were never meant to carry into the next one.

Scripture also does not treat friendship as something owed indefinitely regardless of effort. Proverbs says "A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly" (Proverbs 18:24, KJV), tying friendship to active, ongoing effort rather than a status earned once and kept forever. And "Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend" (Proverbs 27:17, KJV) describes friendship as something that requires the contact and friction of actual, ongoing presence, not a bond that survives on memory alone. None of this diminishes the friendships that were real and are now over. It simply confirms what Dunbar's research also found: closeness was always going to require renewal, and its absence is not proof the friendship was never true.

Not Every Ending Needs a Reason

School friends who made perfect sense inside a shared building and shared schedule, and simply did not have a natural next context once that ended. Coworkers who mattered enormously during a hard season, and quietly stopped needing daily contact once that season passed. That is not failure. Some friendships are built for a season, do their work well within it, and are allowed to end there without either person having done anything wrong.

Grieving Without a Villain

Give yourself permission to miss someone you are not angry at. Permission to feel the absence without needing a story about betrayal to justify feeling it. And permission, too, to reach out if you want to, because sometimes a fading friendship is not a closed door, it is simply a door that needs someone to knock on it again. But if you reach out and the season truly has passed, that is allowed to be true as well, without it meaning either of you failed each other.

Try This This Week

Think of one friendship that faded rather than broke, and choose deliberately what to do with it. Either send one honest, low pressure message reopening the door, with no expectation attached to how it lands, or write a short private note to yourself naming what that friendship gave you while it lasted. Either action turns a vague, lingering absence into something you actually chose, instead of something that just happened to you.

Journal Corner

Which friendships in my life right now are getting the weekly contact research says closeness actually needs, and which ones have quietly slipped without my noticing. What did a friendship I have lost teach me that I still carry, even though the friendship itself is gone. And is there a season currently ending that I have been resisting instead of grieving.

If you are quietly grieving a friendship that just faded instead of one that broke, you are not being dramatic, and you are not imagining a loss that is not real. Some friendships are seasonal by design. You are allowed to love the season it was, and let it be finished. — Nette
