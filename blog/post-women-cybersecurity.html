What I Wish Someone Told Me Before I Started
Studying Cybersecurity

By Nette
August 6, 2026
9 min read

I started studying cybersecurity the way a lot of people probably do, quietly, at night, with no guarantee it would ever turn into anything. I was already working full time as a Route Inspector, already finishing an Information Technology degree, and I added security certifications on top of that because something in me refused to let go of the idea, even when I had no real evidence yet that it would work out. Nobody sat me down and told me what the road actually looked like, or which doors already existed for someone like me to walk through. So here is what I would tell the version of myself who was just starting, and anyone else standing where she was.

Why I Started

Not because I had a computer science background. I did not. Not because someone in my life was already in the field and could show me the way. Nobody was. I started because I kept noticing that the parts of my work I found most interesting, protecting systems, spotting where things could go wrong, thinking in terms of risk and prevention, all pointed toward the same field, and I got tired of admiring it from a distance.

The Numbers Nobody Tells You Going In

Here is what I wish someone had told me plainly before I started, because it would have saved me some unnecessary doubt. Women currently make up somewhere between roughly a fifth and a quarter of the global cybersecurity workforce, depending on whose research you read, up sharply from around eleven percent less than a decade ago. That number is genuinely moving in the right direction, and it is also still low enough that you will likely be one of fewer women in the room for a while. Both of those facts are true at once, and neither one should be the whole story you tell yourself about whether you belong here.

The more encouraging number, one that gets far less attention, comes from ISC2's most recent workforce research: the large majority of women already working in cybersecurity describe the field as a genuinely welcoming career path, and job satisfaction among women in the field has been climbing. The industry also has a well documented, enormous talent shortage, millions of unfilled positions worldwide, which means the door is not closing behind the people already in. It is still wide open, actively looking for people willing to walk through it.

The numbers are not a warning to stay out. They are a map of exactly where the room still needs filling.

Where to Actually Start Learning

If you are standing at the very beginning with no technical background, a foundational certification is the most efficient first step, something like CompTIA's ITF Plus or Security Plus, both built for people without a computer science degree. From there, hands on practice matters more than more theory: platforms like TryHackMe and Hack The Box let you practice real security scenarios in a safe, guided environment, and both have well documented beginner tracks. Cisco's Skills for All and Google's Cybersecurity Certificate are free or low cost structured options if budget is a concern, which for most career changers, it is.

Do not wait until you feel ready to start any of this. Readiness is not really a feeling you arrive at before you begin, it is something that gets built by beginning.

Women Already a Few Steps Ahead, Worth Following

Isolation is where doubt grows loudest, and proximity to people already doing the work is one of the fastest ways to quiet it. A few women worth knowing about as you find your footing: Tanya Janca, known online as SheHacksPurple, built an entire free learning academy around application security after over two decades in the field, and is refreshingly open about the realities of the work. Rachel Tobac, a social engineering expert and chair of the nonprofit Women in Security and Privacy, is a clear, generous voice on how attackers actually think. Tracy Z. Maleeff, known as InfoSecSherpa, made her own unconventional pivot into the field from a background as a librarian, proof that the traditional path is not the only path in.

Organizations and Resources Worth Knowing

Women in CyberSecurity, known as WiCyS, is the largest dedicated nonprofit in the space, offering mentoring, scholarships, and an annual conference built specifically around recruiting and advancing women in the field. The Women CyberSecurity Society runs scholarships, coaching, and an annual International Women in Cyber Day. Women4Cyber operates similarly on a more international scale, and Girls Who Hack and Black Girls Hack both run structured programs aimed at building the pipeline earlier, useful even if you are well past the "girl" in the name, since many of their resources and communities welcome career changers too. For funding specifically, WiCyS, SANS, and ISC2 all run scholarship programs aimed at women pursuing certifications and conference attendance, worth researching before you assume the cost of entry is higher than it actually is.

What I Would Tell Someone Starting Today

Build a sequence instead of staring at the whole mountain: one foundational certification first, then build outward from there toward more specialized ground as your interest sharpens, whether that is analysis, cloud security, or something else entirely. Find people already a few steps ahead of you, even if that just means following their work online. And be honest about the cost. Studying for a technical field while working full time in an unrelated one is genuinely hard, not hard in a way that means you are doing something wrong, just hard in the plain, physical sense of there being fewer hours in the day than you need. Protect the hours you do have for it fiercely, and forgive yourself on the weeks it does not go as planned.

This Is Bigger Than Me

Part of why I am writing this down at all is that I do not want to be the last woman who has to figure this path out mostly alone. I would like, eventually, to be one of the people a younger woman finds when she goes looking for someone a few steps ahead of her, the way I went looking and mostly found silence. If any part of this is you right now, wondering if a field like this has room for someone starting later, from a different background, without the traditional path behind you, I want to be very direct: it does, and the room is actively getting bigger.

Try This This Week

Pick one foundational certification and set a real exam date, even if it is months away, because a date on the calendar turns a vague intention into an actual project. Then find one woman a few steps ahead of you online, follow her work, and send one honest message or comment. You are not asking her to mentor you for free. You are simply breaking the isolation that makes this feel harder than it is.

Journal Corner

A few questions worth sitting with, notebook in hand. What is the actual evidence behind the belief that you are not the right kind of person for this field, and where did that belief first come from. Which of your current skills, from whatever field you are in now, would genuinely transfer into this one if you looked honestly. And what would you attempt this month if you fully believed the room was as open as the numbers actually say it is.

If you are standing where I was, staring at a mountain of unfamiliar material and wondering if you are the wrong kind of person to climb it, you are not. The field needs more people who started exactly where you are standing right now. — Nette
