Boundaries Aren't Unforgiving

By Nette
August 6, 2026
9 min read

Somewhere along the way I picked up a quiet, unexamined belief that forgiving someone obligated me to let them all the way back in, at the same distance, with the same access, as if the offense had simply been erased. So when I forgave, genuinely forgave, and still found myself keeping a certain distance, I felt guilty about it. Like I had done half the work and stopped. Like real forgiveness was supposed to feel like nothing had happened at all. I want to gently take that belief apart, because I do not think it is true, and I do not think it is biblical either.

The Guilt That Comes After You Forgive

This is a specific kind of guilt that almost nobody names directly. You do the hard, internal work of releasing someone, you stop wishing them harm, you stop replaying the offense on a loop, and then you notice you still do not fully trust them, still do not share everything with them, still keep a small, deliberate distance. And a voice creeps in asking if that means you did not really forgive them at all. I want to say clearly: that voice is wrong.

What a Boundary Actually Is

Family systems researcher Murray Bowen spent much of his career studying what he called differentiation of self, the capacity to stay clearly connected to your own thoughts, values, and sense of identity even while remaining in relationship with people who might pull you away from them. Decades of research building on his work consistently link higher differentiation with lower anxiety and healthier relationships. Therapist Nedra Glover Tawwab, who has spent years helping people put this into practice, defines it simply: a boundary is something that keeps you safe and comfortable in your relationships. Not a wall. Not a punishment. A line that tells people, and yourself, what you can offer safely and what you cannot, right now.

"People do not have to like, agree with, or understand your boundaries to respect them." — Nedra Glover Tawwab

How to Actually Set a Healthy Boundary

Setting a boundary is simpler in structure than it feels in the moment, even though the discomfort of doing it is real every time. Start by naming the specific behavior, not the whole person, so the boundary is about an action rather than a character indictment: not you are toxic, but I am not able to discuss that topic with you right now. State it clearly and once, without over explaining or apologizing your way through it, because a boundary buried in justification reads as a request rather than a limit. Attach a real consequence you are actually willing to follow through on, since Tawwab is direct about this: a boundary without a consequence is not a real boundary, it is a wish. And expect discomfort, both yours and theirs, as a normal part of the process rather than a sign you did something wrong. Tawwab notes that guilt is often simply the tell of a beginner at this, not evidence you have overstepped.

You are also allowed to enforce a boundary through your own actions instead of waiting on someone else's cooperation. Muting a conversation, limiting what you share, choosing not to attend something, are all valid ways of protecting your peace even when the other person never agrees to respect the line you have drawn.

Recognizing When Someone Doesn't Respect a Boundary

Some signs are loud: someone hearing your limit and deliberately pushing past it anyway, treating your no as a negotiation rather than an answer. Other signs are quieter and easier to miss. Repeated guilt trips after you say no. Being told you have changed, or gotten cold, simply for protecting your own peace. Having the same conversation about the same overstep more than once, because the first boundary was never actually honored, just temporarily tolerated. A pattern of apologies from them that are never followed by any change in behavior. If you find yourself constantly explaining the same limit to the same person, that repetition is itself the information. A boundary that is respected does not usually need to be repeated more than once or twice.

Tawwab's advice here is direct: you cannot control how someone reacts to a boundary, but you can decide what happens next. That might mean limiting contact. It might, in some cases, mean ending the relationship altogether. Neither of those outcomes cancels out any forgiveness you have already extended. They are simply the natural, wise response to someone showing you, repeatedly, that access to you is not something they intend to handle carefully.

Forgiveness Releases the Debt, It Does Not Reissue the Key

Here is the distinction that finally settled this for me. Forgiveness is about what you release: the desire for repayment, the grip of resentment, the running tally of what they owe you. A boundary is about what you protect: your peace, your trust, your access to your own inner life. You can fully release someone from the debt they owe you and still, wisely, not hand them a key to a house they have already shown you they will not take care of. Those are two different transactions, and scripture never asks you to do only one and call it both.

Scripture Does Not Ask You to Erase Wisdom

Jesus told His followers to be "wise as serpents, and harmless as doves" (Matthew 10:16, KJV), holding both gentleness and discernment together, not asking one to cancel out the other. Proverbs instructs, "Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life" (Proverbs 4:23, KJV), a command to actively guard your inner life, not leave it unguarded as proof of how forgiving you are. Even Jesus, fully God, discerned who He could entrust Himself to, because He knew what was in people (John 2:24-25). If Jesus exercised discernment about access without it being called unforgiving, you are allowed to as well.

Try This This Week

Pick one relationship where you have been quietly resentful instead of clear, and write out the actual boundary in one plain sentence, the way Tawwab suggests: name the behavior, not the person's character, and state what you need instead. You do not have to deliver it this week. Just write it, so it exists somewhere outside your own head as a clear, specific thing rather than a vague, simmering feeling.

Journal Corner

Where in my life have I mistaken access for love, letting someone stay close simply because pulling back felt unkind. What is one boundary I have stated more than once to the same person, and what does that repetition tell me. And what would it cost me to finally attach a real consequence to a boundary I have been restating without enforcing.

If you have forgiven someone and still keep a careful distance, you have not failed at forgiveness. You have simply understood something true: that releasing a person from what they owe you and rebuilding what they broke are two different processes, on two different timelines, and only one of them is required of you today. — Nette
