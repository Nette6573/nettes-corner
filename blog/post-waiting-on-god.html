Waiting on God When the Timeline
Makes No Sense

By Nette
August 6, 2026
8 min read

There is a specific kind of prayer I have prayed more times than I can count lately. Not the eloquent kind. The short, tired kind. God, is this ever going to move. An application sent into what feels like silence. A season of studying and preparing for a door that has not opened yet. A breakthrough I can see the shape of, but not the arrival date. If you have ever done the math on your own life and the math simply did not add up to where you thought you would be by now, this one is for you.

When the Silence Feels Like a No

Here is what I have had to be honest with myself about: silence from God does not automatically mean no. It often just means not yet, and not yet is one of the hardest sentences in the entire Christian life, because it asks you to keep showing up to something that has not resolved. It is easier, in a strange way, to grieve a closed door than to keep standing in front of one that has not opened or closed, just held.

What Scripture Actually Says About Waiting

Scripture does not treat waiting as a minor inconvenience on the way to the real promise. It treats waiting as its own substantial territory, with its own instructions. "But they that wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint" (Isaiah 40:31, KJV). Notice what that verse does not promise. It does not promise the wait will be short. It promises strength inside the waiting, not just relief at the end of it.

Psalm 27 goes further, almost insisting on it twice in one breath: "Wait on the Lord: be of good courage, and he shall strengthen thine heart: wait, I say, on the Lord" (Psalm 27:14, KJV). And Lamentations, a book written in the middle of genuine devastation, still lands on this: "The Lord is good unto them that wait for him, to the soul that seeketh him... it is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the Lord" (Lamentations 3:25-26, KJV). If waiting could be affirmed as good in the middle of Lamentations, it can survive whatever season you are currently in.

Waiting is not the absence of the promise. It is the space the promise is being built in.

Waiting Is Not Passive

One thing I have had to correct in my own thinking is the idea that waiting on God means doing nothing. Habakkuk, standing watch and waiting for an answer, was told: "Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables, that he may run that readeth it... though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come" (Habakkuk 2:2-3, KJV). He was not told to sit still. He was told to prepare, to write it down, to get ready for an arrival he had not seen yet. Biblical waiting looks a lot less like standing frozen and a lot more like studying at night, submitting the application anyway, showing up to the interview, doing the next faithful thing you can actually see, while the part you cannot control stays in God's hands.

What to Actually Do While You Wait

A few practices that have kept me from spiraling in the in between. Writing the vision down the way Habakkuk was told to, literally, in a notebook or a note on my phone, so the goal stays specific instead of dissolving into vague anxiety. Serving someone else in the middle of my own waiting, because turning outward, even briefly, interrupts the loop of checking my phone or my inbox for news that has not come yet. Staying obedient in the small, unglamorous things I can actually control today, the study session, the application, the conversation, instead of waiting for the big breakthrough to justify the small effort. And building a rhythm of gratitude for what has already been provided, because rehearsing what God has already done is often what steadies me enough to keep trusting Him with what He has not done yet.

Joseph waited roughly thirteen years between the dream he was given as a teenager and the moment it was actually fulfilled, through betrayal, slavery, and prison, with long stretches where nothing visibly moved at all. Scripture does not record him doing nothing in that time. It records him working with excellence wherever he was placed, even in a prison cell, long before he understood why. That is the pattern worth copying: full effort where you are, full trust for what you cannot yet see.

When Waiting Turns Into Doubt

I want to be honest instead of tidy about this part. There are days the waiting does not feel spiritual at all. It feels like frustration, like comparing your timeline to everyone else's, like wondering if you misheard the whole direction you thought you were supposed to be moving in. Scripture does not pretend that struggle away either. The Psalms are full of people demanding to know how long, Lord, how long, and being met with patience instead of rebuke for asking. Bringing God your impatience is not a failure of faith. Pretending you do not feel it usually is closer to the actual problem.

Try This This Week

Do what Habakkuk was told to do. Write the specific thing you are waiting on, in plain, concrete language, somewhere you will actually see it again. Then next to it, write one small act of obedience you can complete this week regardless of whether the answer comes, something within your control, so the waiting has an active shape instead of only a passive one.

Journal Corner

What am I most tempted to force open on my own timeline right now, and what would trusting God's timing actually look like this week, in practical terms. Where have I seen God come through before, and how does remembering that change how I feel about today's silence. And what would I do differently this week if I fully believed the wait was not empty.

If your timeline currently makes no sense to you, you are in good, ancient company. The waiting is not empty space between where you are and where you are going. It is where the strength Isaiah promised is quietly being built, whether you can feel it happening yet or not. — Nette
